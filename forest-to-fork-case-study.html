Where it started

The first job was a single edit: 48 minutes of footage from a sambar hunt, cut down to a 27-minute film. Luke had shot it for himself, not for a camera crew, and said so. The channel had two short videos on it.

The footage was honest and the hunting was good. What was missing was a way to turn a working guide's weekends into a steady stream of content without asking him to become a filmmaker.

How the work runs

Luke is in the NSW bush and we are a long way from it. The system below is how a hunt becomes a published episode without anyone getting on a plane.

  1. Before a hunt, Luke gets a shot list with reference frames: the walk in, glassing, camp, the stalk, the carry out. He films on his phone, a GoPro and a drone.
  2. After the hunt, he uploads the footage and sends a voice-note recap of what happened, morning by morning. That recap becomes the spine of the edit.
  3. We cut, grade and subtitle the episode, and Luke checks every cut for ethics. Anything that shows a poor shot or invites the wrong reading comes out.
  4. Captions and descriptions are drafted in Luke's voice and he corrects them in his own words. Nothing goes out sounding like an agency.
  5. Episodes publish on a fixed calendar, with thumbnails tested by YouTube against each other. When Luke is out of reception, the calendar still holds.

Alongside the videos we rebuilt the brand basics: one handle across every platform, a cleaned-up logo and tagline (A Timeless Pursuit), his licensed-guide credentials on the site, and a brand film for the homepage. Clients became guests in every piece of copy.

What changed

The website stepped up after we took over the channel in August 2025 and again once the brand film went on the homepage. YouTube only took off once the diary series gave it a steady release calendar in March 2026. From August 2025 to February 2026 the channel published 7 videos, two of them long-form. From March to early October 2026 it published 15, eleven of them long-form.

What we learned

Honest hunts outperform polished ones

The best episodes lead with what went wrong. "2 Days, 1 Clean Miss, 1 Heart Shot" is the most watched diary episode. "Close Encounter", a bow hunt that ended without a harvest, is close behind. "I Broke My Own Rule", where Luke owns a shot he should not have taken, is the fastest video the channel has had. Hunters can tell when a video is sanding the edges off.

Consistency builds a floor

The median day on the channel went from 5 engaged views to 63. The back catalogue now earns between releases, so each new episode starts from a higher base.

Every format has a job

Hunt diaries hold the existing audience and earn the watch time. Gear and education videos bring in people searching for answers. Shorts buy reach, but they are 14 of the channel's 33 videos and only 5% of its watch time. The brand film converts viewers to subscribers better than anything else.

Google books the hunts, YouTube earns the trust

YouTube sends almost nobody straight to the website. People watch Luke guide, then search for him later. One guest found Forest to Fork through a diary episode, booked a bow hunt, and became an episode himself. That loop is the whole idea.

What comes next

More people now find Forest to Fork than ever, but visits have grown faster than bookings. Most people who start the enquiry form do not finish it, and committing to a multi-day hunt is a big first step. The next phase is conversion: an easier first step than "book a hunt", a rebuilt website, a wall of guests with their harvests and their own words, and more Google reviews.
